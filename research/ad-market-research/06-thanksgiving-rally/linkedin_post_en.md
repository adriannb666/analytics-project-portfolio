Is the "Santa Claus rally" real? I tested it on 76 years of S&P 500 data.

Every year around Thanksgiving you hear the same story: the last weeks of the year are the best time to own stocks. There is also a scarier one: "If Santa Claus should fail to call, bears may come to Broad and Wall."

I pulled daily S&P 500 closes from 1950 to 2025 and compared each window with 10,000 random windows of the same length.

1) The Santa rally is real.
The last 5 trading days of December plus the first 2 of January averaged +1.25%. A random 7-day window averaged +0.26%. Only 3 of 10,000 simulations did as well (p ≈ 0.0003). Up in 58 of 76 years.

2) The wider Thanksgiving-to-year-end run is weaker.
+1.73% vs +0.88% for random windows (p ≈ 0.04). Most of the extra return comes from those last 7 days, plus the Wednesday before Thanksgiving and Black Friday. The Monday after Thanksgiving gives about half of it back (−0.29% on average, up in only 38% of years).

3) It's fading.
In the 2010s the Santa rally averaged +0.30%, about the same as any 7 trading days (+0.32%). In 2020–25: +0.28% vs +0.40%. It has failed three years in a row.

4) "Santa fails, bears come" doesn't hold.
After the 17 failed Santa rallies, the S&P still rose the next year 12 times. Santa failed at the end of 2023 and 2024; the index then gained 23.3% and 16.4%.

5) Real, but tiny.
Holding the S&P only during those 7 days each year turned $1 into $2.52 since 1950. Buy-and-hold turned it into $411 (price only, no dividends).

My takeaway: a seasonal effect can be statistically real and still useless for timing. If anything, the practical lesson runs the other way: history gives no reason to sell in December.
